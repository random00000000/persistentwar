# Persistent War Officer Fork Intent

## Purpose

This document locks the intent for a future clone or fork of the current `Top Down Extraction Shooter` project.

It is not a replacement for the current tactical squad extraction north star. It exists so a later project can deliberately pivot from extraction-first play into a Foxhole-like persistent war game while preserving the strongest existing ingredients:

- readable top-down gunplay
- strong gun identity
- emergent soldier dialogue
- slow tactical fights
- stash and operation preparation
- living battlefield consequence

The future fork should be treated as its own product direction.

## Product Statement

The fork should become a top-down persistent war game where the player acts as an officer who shapes the battlefield through commands, build orders, logistics, and personal intervention.

The player does not primarily win by personally killing everyone.

The player wins by making decisions that cause soldiers to live, die, hold, retreat, resupply, build, and break.

The core promise is:

`Your orders are the reason people live and die.`

The game should feel like an early Foxhole-style war slice, but single-player and NPC-driven first:

- two sides
- one replayable town
- two opposing camps
- slow battles
- autonomous soldiers
- officer build orders
- protected stash preparation
- one match ends when either camp is destroyed

Online play can come much later. The first version should prove the NPC war.

## Core Fantasy

You are an officer in a persistent battlefield.

You place orders:

- build this trench
- reinforce this camp
- stock this ammo crate
- hold this road
- suppress this house
- push this field
- fall back to this line
- recover the wounded
- bring up supplies

Soldiers then perform those orders inside a living battlefield.

They do not act like instant RTS pieces. They behave like people in a slow, dangerous war:

- they walk to the work site
- they carry supplies
- they dig under risk
- they seek cover
- they get pinned
- they suppress
- they retreat
- they drag wounded
- they panic or hesitate
- they hold because the order and terrain gave them a chance

The main action is not clicking enemies.

The main action is placing buildings, issuing intent, and watching the war unfold.

## Genre Position

This should not become a pure command-console RTS.

It should be closer to:

`Foxhole + RollerCoaster Tycoon-style NPC simulation + hardcore top-down war intervention.`

The RollerCoaster Tycoon lesson matters:

If the genre is about watching agents react to a constructed environment, then rich NPC behavior is not over-engineering. It is the game.

For this fork, soldier behavior is the attraction.

The player should care because the battlefield systems make individual NPC decisions visible, dramatic, and consequential.

## Modern Ukraine-War Context

The product should be grounded in the content niche created by the modern Ukraine war without becoming a literal recreation of real units, real towns, real atrocities, or real current operations.

The audience interest is clear:

- people watch trench assaults because the tactical problem is legible and terrifying
- people watch drone footage because one cheap tool can change a whole local fight
- people follow logistics because artillery shells, fuel, trucks, generators, and batteries decide whether a line lives
- people remember the weird details: tape colors, civilian vehicles, improvised armor, muddy trenches, abandoned villages, thermal clips, radios, field repairs, and small teams surviving inside ruined ground
- people are drawn to the contrast between industrial-scale war and individual soldiers making tiny life-or-death decisions

The game should understand this niche and translate it into playable, systemic fiction.

The target is not propaganda.

The target is:

`a fictional modern attritional war where trenches, drones, logistics, artillery threat, field construction, and exhausted soldiers create emergent stories.`

## Fictionalization Rule

The setting should be fictional even when the inspiration is obvious.

Use the real-world context to understand battlefield texture:

- trench systems
- shell-crater fields
- drone observation
- FPV drone threat
- artillery and mortar fear
- mine and wire belts
- improvised civilian vehicles
- logistics trucks
- small assault groups
- blue/yellow/green tape-style identification
- ruined villages
- tree-line fighting
- basement and bunker living
- generator, battery, radio, and Starlink-like communications dependence
- medical evacuation under fire

Do not directly recreate:

- real unit names
- real commanders
- real ongoing battles
- real massacre sites
- real propaganda slogans
- real political claims as game truth
- real civilians as exploitable content

The fictional war can borrow the shape of modern frontline combat while keeping its own factions, places, symbols, and history.

## Battlefield Texture To Capture

The game should feel like soldiers are fighting inside a damaged, improvised, constantly watched landscape.

Important texture:

- muddy trench networks that matter more than open heroics
- houses used as fighting positions until they become death traps
- basements used as barracks, aid stations, and last holds
- tree lines that hide movement but invite artillery or drone attention
- roads that look useful but become kill zones
- civilian cars and vans converted into transport
- burned vehicles left as permanent map scars
- ammo piles, fuel, batteries, radios, and generators as real war resources
- soldiers marking themselves with tape or armbands because friendly-fire confusion is a real battlefield problem
- small assault groups that clear one trench, one shed, or one house at a time
- long quiet waits broken by sudden violence
- bad weather, mud, smoke, dust, and darkness changing the value of orders

The player should feel that the war is not clean.

The front is improvised, observed, dangerous, and always hungry for supplies.

## Modern War Systems To Translate Into Gameplay

The Ukraine-war context should become systems, not only flavor text.

### Trenches And Fieldworks

Trenches are not decorative cover.

They are the main terrain technology of the war.

Gameplay implications:

- soldiers strongly prefer trenches under fire
- trench direction matters
- a badly placed trench can trap a retreat
- unfinished trenches create exposed builder drama
- trench junctions become small tactical problems
- grenades, suppression, and flanking decide trench fights

### Drones And Observation

Drones should eventually become a battlefield pressure layer.

Gameplay implications:

- exposed movement can be spotted
- build sites can become known targets
- artillery or mortar risk can increase after observation
- drone operators, batteries, and radio links become valuable
- destroying or jamming observation changes the fight

Day-one does not need full drone simulation, but the design should leave room for it.

### Artillery, Mortars, And Indirect Threat

Indirect fire should make static success temporary.

Gameplay implications:

- strong positions attract fire
- clustered soldiers are at risk
- camp storage and ammo dumps can cook off
- shelling changes terrain with craters and ruined cover
- players must choose between concentration and dispersion

### Logistics

Logistics should be the bloodstream of the game.

Gameplay implications:

- ammo crates decide whether a line keeps firing
- construction supplies decide whether orders can be completed
- fuel decides whether vehicles and generators matter
- batteries and communications gear enable drone or command systems
- med supplies decide whether wounded become survivors or bodies

The audience for this niche understands that logistics is not a side activity. Logistics is the war.

### Small Assault Groups

The combat should favor small, readable groups rather than giant blob armies.

Gameplay implications:

- two to six soldiers can decide a local fight
- one machine gun can stop a road
- one trench section can save a camp
- one officer order can collapse or save a push
- two coordinated players should eventually be able to end a match

### Identification And Confusion

The game should use fictional tape/armband colors to make sides readable while preserving the modern battlefield feel.

Gameplay implications:

- faction identity is visual and practical
- smoke, darkness, distance, and panic can reduce clarity
- friendly-fire risk can exist later if the simulation supports it
- captured gear can create useful but risky ambiguity

## Content Niche And Audience Promise

This fork should aim at players who are fascinated by:

- Foxhole-style persistence
- top-down tactical shooters
- NPC simulation games
- logistics and base-building games
- trench warfare footage
- small-unit modern combat stories
- emergent RimWorld-style drama
- slow cinematic battle observation

The promise to that audience is:

`Build the line, supply it, order soldiers into danger, watch the fight evolve, and know that the outcome came from your decisions.`

The game should create clips and stories people want to share:

- a trench finished under fire
- a camp saved by one ammo run
- a doomed assault caused by bad wire placement
- a bunker held until supplies ran out
- a wounded builder dragged out by another soldier
- a personally deployed officer dying and losing tech progress
- two players coordinating enough build and assault orders to destroy the enemy camp

## Tone Guardrails

The product can be grounded in modern war without becoming careless.

Rules:

- keep factions fictional
- keep the conflict fictional
- avoid real atrocity reenactment
- avoid treating civilian suffering as a reward loop
- let civilians, if present, create moral and logistical pressure rather than points
- portray death, exhaustion, fear, and loss as consequences, not comedy alone
- allow dark humor among soldiers, but keep it tied to character stress and survival
- do not make one side cartoonishly pure or cartoonishly evil

The fantasy is not clean heroism.

The fantasy is command responsibility inside a grinding fictional war.

## Player Role

The player is primarily an officer and battlefield shaper.

The player can also enter the war personally, but that should be dangerous and costly.

Officer-layer actions:

- place build orders
- choose where trenches, bunkers, wire, depots, med posts, and firing positions go
- assign squads or let soldiers self-organize around needs
- prioritize supply, construction, defense, assault, or recovery
- launch operations from stash
- decide when to personally enter the battlefield

Soldier-layer actions:

- direct movement and shooting using the existing top-down gun feel
- fight with real weapon identity
- loot and recover valuable equipment
- influence key moments personally
- risk death and progression loss

The player should feel powerful because they shape the war, not because they are invincible.

## Slow And Cinematic Battles

Battles should be slow, readable, and almost real.

Slow does not mean boring. It means fights have time to breathe and evolve:

- first contact
- soldiers dive into cover
- suppression starts
- one side probes
- a squad backs off
- wounded call out
- someone tries to build or repair under fire
- a machine gun pins a road
- a trench becomes the reason a line survives
- an ammo crate running dry changes the fight
- a bad order causes a collapse

AI should be allowed to back off from a fight.

AI should be allowed to get pinned.

AI should be allowed to choose cover over aggression.

AI should be allowed to suppress instead of rushing.

AI should be allowed to lose nerve, recover, regroup, and try again.

This is the opposite of arcade swarm combat.

The desired fight is cinematic because the chain of cause and effect is visible.

## Build Orders As The Main Verb

The most important player action is placing battlefield infrastructure.

Every building must have a risk and a reward.

Examples:

- A trench protects soldiers, but exposes builders while it is being dug.
- A bunker anchors a line, but attracts artillery, raids, or focused attacks.
- An ammo crate extends a defense, but becomes a valuable target.
- A med post saves wounded, but creates traffic and recovery pressure.
- Barbed wire slows assaults, but can trap friendlies during a retreat.
- A roadblock buys time, but can also block logistics.
- A forward depot enables operations, but risks losing stored materiel.

Buildings should not appear instantly.

The player places the order. Soldiers must execute it.

That execution creates drama:

- Do they reach the site?
- Do they have materials?
- Are they protected?
- Do they finish before the enemy push?
- Was the position worth the lives spent building it?

## Soldiers As The Center Of The Simulation

Soldiers should be the heart of the game.

They should react to:

- cover
- trenches
- suppression
- nearby deaths
- nearby officers
- ammo availability
- med support
- orders
- morale
- line of sight
- enemy pressure
- camp state
- retreat paths

They should be able to:

- occupy trenches
- prefer firing ports and sandbags
- carry materials
- build assigned structures
- resupply from crates
- rescue wounded
- drag bodies or wounded under fire
- suppress enemy positions
- assault when a position is softened
- retreat when isolated
- surrender or collapse when overwhelmed

The goal is not random chaos.

The goal is readable emergent drama.

## Emergent Storytelling

Emergent storytelling should be rich in drama.

Drama should come from simulation truth, not fake flavor pasted on top.

Good stories should sound like:

- `The trench finished ten seconds before the assault hit.`
- `The ammo crate was too far back and the forward line collapsed.`
- `A medic died trying to drag a builder out of the open.`
- `The machine gun team held the road because the bunker faced the right way.`
- `The player ordered a forward depot and it became the battle's main target.`
- `A bad wire placement trapped the retreat.`
- `The officer entered the fight personally, died, and lost tech progress.`

Dialogue should react to these truths.

Soldiers should comment on:

- bad orders
- miracle holds
- friends dying
- exposed builds
- empty ammo
- successful trenches
- camp collapse
- night pushes
- officer death
- hard-won recovery

The tone should remain grounded, tense, human, and sometimes darkly funny.

## Hardcore Personal Intervention

The player can enter the war personally.

This should be tempting but dangerous.

When the player enters the battlefield:

- the game uses the existing top-down shooter strengths
- guns remain distinct and meaningful
- personal skill can decide a key fight
- death matters

Hardcore rule:

`If the officer dies in the war, they lose tech tree progress.`

This makes personal intervention a real decision.

The officer can save a fight, but risking the officer should never be casual.

## Stash And Extraction Menu

The extraction and stash layer should remain in the fork, but its purpose changes.

In the current game, extraction locks in raid survival.

In this fork, stash and extraction support persistent war preparation.

Players like to grind, store equipment, and prepare future operations. The protected stash should let players preserve:

- tanks
- good guns
- rare supplies
- construction materials
- specialist kits
- operation stockpiles

The stash is protected so other players or enemy systems cannot simply steal everything while the player is away.

This matters especially for later online play.

The stash should answer:

- what did I save from the war?
- what can I deploy tomorrow?
- what tanks or weapons are protected?
- what operation can I afford next?
- what tech did I risk by entering personally?

The extraction menu should support the grind-and-bank fantasy without turning the game back into a pure extraction shooter.

## First Town Slice

The first playable target is one replayable, expandable town.

The town should be the whole product slice, not a throwaway test arena.

It contains:

- one camp for Side A
- one camp for Side B
- a contested town between them
- roads and supply paths
- buildable defensive lines
- a small number of meaningful buildings
- enough space for trenches, bunkers, depots, and firefights

The match ends when one camp is destroyed.

This echoes the early Foxhole promise:

`Two people should be able to meaningfully end the match.`

The first slice should support a small-scale war where:

- one player can shape the whole front
- two players could eventually coordinate and finish the match
- NPC soldiers make the battlefield feel alive even before online play

## First Town Requirements

The first town must be replayable and expandable.

Replayable means:

- different build layouts create different fights
- different supply plans change battle tempo
- trench placement matters
- camp attack routes vary
- NPC behavior creates different stories
- the town does not feel solved after one win

Expandable means:

- later buildings can be added without replacing the town
- later vehicles can use the same roads and depots
- later online play can reuse the same camp structure
- later tech progression can unlock new construction and operation options

The first town should include:

- two camps
- one central road
- one side route
- at least one open field
- at least one dense building cluster
- at least one trench-friendly approach
- at least one logistics route
- at least one protected stash/deployment edge

## Win And Loss

The first match win condition is simple:

`Destroy the enemy camp.`

The loss condition is:

`Your camp is destroyed.`

The camp should be more than a health bar.

It should include:

- command core
- storage
- spawn or reinforcement function
- supply stock
- build capability

Damaging a camp should reduce its ability to sustain the war before it fully dies.

## Tech Tree Progress

The fork should include tech progression, but it must be tied to risk.

Tech can unlock:

- better trenches
- bunkers
- improved logistics
- vehicles
- tanks
- heavier guns
- better medical infrastructure
- better command tools

The officer's personal death should cost tech progress.

This keeps the officer from becoming a disposable combat unit.

The protected stash lets players preserve physical assets, but tech progression remains emotionally dangerous.

## Design Pillars

### 1. Officer Consequence

The player's orders create life and death outcomes.

A bad order should be visible in the battlefield.

### 2. NPC War First

The game must work as an NPC-driven war before online play exists.

Online should amplify the system later, not rescue it.

### 3. Slow Cinematic Combat

Fights should develop through suppression, cover, retreat, construction, logistics, and morale.

### 4. Building Risk And Reward

Every structure should be useful, dangerous, and consequential.

### 5. Protected Grind

The stash should let players bank tanks, good guns, and supplies for future operations.

### 6. Personal Intervention Is Hardcore

The player can enter the war, but death costs tech progress.

### 7. First Town First

Prove one replayable town before broadening into a larger war.

## What Carries Over From The Current Project

The fork should carry forward:

- weapon identity and gun feel
- top-down readable combat
- suppression concepts
- soldier dialogue and memory
- squad/boys emotional texture
- stash preparation
- extraction/banking flow
- territorial state ideas
- casualty and recovery drama
- route/scar memory

The fork should not carry forward extraction as the primary win condition.

Extraction becomes one support layer inside a larger persistent war.

## What Changes From The Current Project

The current game is:

`tactical squad extraction first`

The fork is:

`persistent officer-led war simulation first`

The current player question is:

`Can we survive this operation and extract with value?`

The fork player question is:

`What should I build, order, risk, and personally enter so my side survives and the enemy camp falls?`

## First Prototype Target

The first prototype should prove:

`Can placing a trench, ammo crate, or bunker materially change how NPC soldiers fight and whether a camp survives?`

Minimum prototype:

- one town
- two camps
- NPC soldiers on both sides
- officer build-order mode
- trench build order
- ammo crate build order
- simple camp destruction win condition
- soldier cover seeking
- soldier suppression
- soldier construction behavior
- protected stash shell
- officer personal deployment with death penalty placeholder

This is enough to prove the new game.

Do not start with:

- giant map
- full multiplayer
- deep economy
- many towns
- complex vehicle production
- large tech tree

Those come after one town produces real stories.

## Durable Rule

Evaluate future features with this question:

`Does this make the player feel like an officer whose build orders and commands shape a slow, cinematic NPC war where people live or die because of those decisions?`

If the answer is no, it is probably not core fork work.
